## Overview

Workers' compensation coverage is mandatory in every state except Texas, where
it is optional but strongly advised. Premiums are driven by payroll, industry
classification codes, and the employer's loss history (experience modifier).

## California

California requires coverage for nearly all employees, including part-time and
seasonal workers. The state operates a competitive market: private carriers and
the State Compensation Insurance Fund (SCIF) both write policies. Minimum
premiums are low but audit exposure is high — payroll misclassification is the
most common source of mid-term premium adjustments. California does not allow
employers to use a pay-as-you-go plan below a $25,000 annual premium threshold.

## Texas

Texas is the only state where workers' compensation is elective. Employers who
opt out are "non-subscribers" and face civil liability exposure in workplace
injury lawsuits. Non-subscribers must post notice to employees. Agents should
position coverage as asset protection rather than compliance.

## New York

New York requires coverage from the first employee, including owners in most
cases. The state's statutory disability benefits law (DBL) sits alongside
workers' comp and is frequently missed by first-time employers. Quarterly
payroll reports are mandatory and late filings trigger penalties.

## Florida

Florida mandates coverage for employers with four or more employees. Sole
proprietors and LLC members may elect coverage. The state uses an automated
construction classification system; misclassification of roofing versus
framing work is a frequent audit finding.

## Illinois

Illinois requires coverage for all employers with one or more employees.
The state runs a competitive assigned-risk pool, and experience modification
factors follow the NCCI. Employer-paid healthcare plans do not replace
statutory benefits.

## Kentucky

Kentucky requires coverage at four or more employees. The state imposes
strict deadlines for filing injury claims — 30 days from the date of injury.
Employers who fail to secure coverage face stop-work orders and civil
penalties.
